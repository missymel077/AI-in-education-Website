To What Extent Does AI Affect the Education sector?

Introduction to AI

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is making its way into the educational industry and altering how students learn, and teachers teach (Luckin, 2018). With the major developments in AI-powered tools, we see how AI-powered revision websites, personalised learning platforms, AI-created assessments and many others are changing the traditional learning methods to what can be perceived as highly interactive and smarter techniques of learning through the usage of AI (Holmes, Bialik and Fadel, 2019). These new advancements in technology assist students by allowing them more self-sufficiency as learners while also providing teachers with a new means to act in response to a wide spectrum of learning needs. Given how AI-powered tools are continuing to shape, change and evolve, the educational sector needs to be able to adapt along with it in order to ensure the best learning experiences are possible within this more digitalised world (Selwyn, 2019).

Overview of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Education

The phrase artificial intelligence (AI) describes systems that are capable of performing tasks that would normally involve human intelligence. Some examples of human abilities that they can mimic include but are also not limited to are cognitive ability, learning and decision-making (Woolf, 2010). AI is becoming extremely more present in education. It has already trickled its way into a variety of areas, this includes virtual tutors, adaptive learning and automated marking software. These tools can be utilised to help teachers to be able to customise their styles of how they teach specific subjects to individual students, this can create a more personalised and tangible learning experience (McKinsey & Company, 2020) Artificial Intelligence can be used to track students as they work through study materials so that teachers can identify exactly where they struggle and when, and then offer learning material that fits their learning style, as well as their learning schedule. Furthermore, to utilise their natural learning skills, AI can analyse large amounts of student performance data to recognise issues and predict outcomes, so this means at-risk students can receive the support they need (Holmes, Bialik and Fadel, 2019).

The introduction of AI in the education sector has almost certainly had a plethora of benefits. Some of these benefits include improved efficiency, accessibility, and engagement (Luckin, 2018). Nowadays, AI can help the growing need to escape traditional methods of teaching which often has the main concept of one-size-fits-all. (Selwyn, 2019) AI-driven platforms are able to provide students with feedback and learning support during a learning activity. AI can be helpful for students with disabilities. AI is able to adapt to the difference in learning experiences this encourages habits among students to learn independently, therefore in turn enabling them to take a higher level of responsibility for their own academic progress and performance (Woolf, 2010).The potential AI has to enhance student engagement and motivation by making learning more interactive and stimulating. AI-driven quizzes, AI- made exams, and virtual reality experiences that create an educational environment that harnesses students’ interest and promotes more active participation (Luckin, 2018). Impact of AI on Learning

Although AI has a numerous amount of benefits, I find important that we also consider the amount of impacts it has on different areas of the education sector, such as its impacts on teaching methods, student independence and the lost of critical thinking.

Dependence on AI and Potential Loss of Critical Thinking Skills

While AI-driven learning systems provide convenience, they also lead to concerns regarding overdependence on technology. With students relying on AI tools to solve problems these days, students are losing the ability to think critically or solve a problem on their own. Applications such as Grammarly correct grammar errors on the spot, but students might not grasp the old language rules. Even more, AI-based calculators and tools such as Wolfram Alpha can quickly give us a solution to complex problems but do not show us the process of solving them. This dependency undermines the spirit of enquiry and critical thinking. There's also the problem of passive learning. Because these AI platforms process the student through different steps a narrow defined set of process. More important, with this, students will be accustomed to being told answers instead of going out there to find it themselves and it will discourage them from facing problems they have never encountered before. Excessive reliance on AI is also a threat to creativity. Students can only approach problem-scenario questions based on the predefined algorithms. This can produce a homogeneity of ideology, stifling creativity and the spirit of innovation. While teachers are essential in developing critical thinkers, their role may also be reduced in the AI classroom. When a digital assistant like Bard or ChatGPT is the main interlocutor in the class, students miss years of conversation, contention, and activities that shape reasoning. Education without human contact might lead to a long-term effect on cognitive and social development. Solving these issues is not a polarity but rather a equilibrium. Schools have to introduce AI in ways that they engage in active learning. Instead of giving solutions, for instance, AI tools should ask students to describe their rationale. Using gamified platforms will open opportunities for problem-solving exercises that allow them to experiment and be creative. Focus on using critical thinking to solve real-world problems while still keeping teachers as the centre of the learning process. To offset the more passive nature of AI-driven learning, institutions can use collaborative endeavours and group discussions. The key is to still prioritise independent thinking within students and appropriate applicants, and those responsible for education and public policy must work hard to support such a goal. Though AI has improved education, if students become too reliant on technology, they may not be able to cope with the challenge of solving problems without it, only relying on an AI if they are stumped.

Ethical and Social Considerations of AI in Education

Artificial Intelligence can provide a wide range of opportunities in the field of education, however, there are also emerging ethical and social issues that must be considered (Selwyn, 2019). Data privacy, algorithmic bias, and the digital divide are just a few of the controversial issues being raised. Thus, these are vital to the effectiveness and equity of use of such AI-driven education (Luckin 2018). The collection of student data for analysis by AI systems has led to such questions about the storage, sharing and security of personal data. AI algorithms risk entrenching implicit biases present in the organisations (Holmes, Bialik, and Fadel 2019) which disadvantage certain students groups. Moreover, students become over dependant on AI which makes them not put in as much effort for things like homework or essays, it also creates a rift as not everyone has access to sophisticated technology and high-speed internet(bakadi, 2020). The ability to deal with these alarming issues is the key to ensuring that AI optimises educational experiences without sacrificing ethical principles or further increasing the disparity between privileged and disadvantaged students.

Impact on Traditional Teaching Methods

AI is reshaping traditional education. The need for one-size-fits-all instruction is reduced by AI tools that deliver personalised learning experiences. While this benefits students, it raises questions about the role of teachers in the future (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2017). AI systems like Squirrel AI and Carnegie Learning provide tailored content more effectively than a teacher managing a large classroom. These systems adjust lesson materials based on a student's ability and provide instant feedback using real-time performance data (Luckin ., 2016). This level of personalisation is difficult for teachers to replicate at scale. AI also automates tasks traditionally handled by teachers, such as grading and assessments. Tools like Gradescope can evaluate exams quickly and accurately, reducing the time teachers spend on administrative tasks (Holmes ., 2019). Over time, schools might view AI as a cost-effective alternative to human instructors. Virtual classrooms and AI-powered tutors further diminish the need for traditional teaching methods. Students can access lessons and guidance on-demand, minimising reliance on fixed schedules. This flexibility is appealing to institutions seeking to modernise education (Holmes ., 2019). However, the importance of teachers in fostering emotional and social development cannot be overlooked. AI lacks the ability to understand emotions or provide moral guidance. Students benefit from mentorship and interpersonal relationships that technology cannot replicate (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2017). AI should complement, not replace, teachers. By automating repetitive tasks, AI allows teachers to focus on mentoring, creativity, and addressing students' emotional needs. Striking a balance ensures traditional teaching methods remain relevant in an AI-driven educational.

Conclusion

AI in education has changed the nature of how students of this generation study, learn and even revise. Though AI has definitely had some positive effects, the ethical concerns do come hand in hand when it comes to AI conveying its full impact.
